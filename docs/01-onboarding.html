Document 01

Onboarding

What this part of the lab does, and guidance for how to get started.

§1 Overview of the computational wing of the Raredon Lab

The computational wing develops tools, methods, and analyses to investigate cell-to-cell signaling and tissue-scale phenomena — the patterns and behaviors that emerge from many interacting cells and cannot be understood by studying single molecules or single cells in isolation. The work spans tissue biology, tissue engineering, and computational biology, and is built to be generalizable: methods and findings should extend readily to other tissues, other labs, and other questions beyond those immediately under study. A defining commitment is using visualization to make structure legible — understanding what is in a dataset before measuring it, then developing purpose-built visuals to communicate findings to computational and non-computational colleagues alike. Much of the lab's data is graph-structured, or can be meaningfully formalized as a graph, and this geometric lens shapes how tools are built and results are interpreted. Computational scientists here serve the larger wet-lab and clinical team by translating biological measurements into rigorous, reproducible, shareable quantitative insight. The work demands sustained curiosity, careful habits of documentation, and the willingness to explain what you are doing in plain language to anyone who asks.

§2 Why this manual exists

A computational lab is as complex as a wet lab. It has protocols, best practices, and standards that are just as essential to producing high-quality, reproducible, broadly applicable science. The practices described here make the work more durable: easier for collaborators to use, easier for reviewers to trust, and easier for future scientists to build on.

Writing these practices down is also a statement of respect for the people who join this lab. Standards passed on informally are uneven, easily forgotten, and impossible to improve collectively. A written record gives everyone the same foundation to work from, and something concrete to update as tools and methods evolve.

Read this manual once, front to back, when you join. Confirm with the PI that you have done so, and bring any questions to your second 1:1. Refer back to specific sections whenever you need a reminder, and use it to orient others when the time comes. The manual will be revised over time as the lab's practices develop.

§3 Your first week

The following checklist covers the practical setup that should be complete by the end of your first week. Work through it in roughly this order and ask for help whenever you need it.

  1. 1
    Read this manual

    Start here, front to back. Note any questions as you go. You do not need to memorize it; you need a map of what is here and where to find it.

  2. 2
    Set up your computing environment

    Follow Document 02 to install required software and configure your machine and HPC access. Do not skip steps or defer items: a properly configured environment is the foundation for everything else.

  3. 3
    Schedule your recurring 1:1 with the PI

    Set up a weekly meeting with the PI. This is your primary channel for scientific direction, project feedback, and discussing blockers. Establish it in the first week so it becomes part of your routine immediately.

  4. 4
    Get your calendar and communications set up

    Configure your Yale Outlook calendar. Make sure you are invited to all recurring lab meetings and added to the relevant mailing lists. Join the lab Slack workspace, the primary channel for day-to-day questions and coordination.

  5. 5
    Meet with a senior lab member to complete your setup

    Schedule a dedicated setup meeting with the senior computational scientist in the lab. This session covers HPC access, data object locations, lab-specific workflows, and any setup items not covered by the general computing environment document.

  6. 6
    Get access to the relevant data

    Identify the primary datasets you will be working with and confirm access. Large data objects live on the lab HPCs, the Raredon Lab server, or the RaredonLab SharePoint. Know which applies to your project before you need the data for the first time.

  7. 7
    Review the setup checklist with the PI

    At your second 1:1 meeting, walk through the setup checklist together. This is also the time to ask any questions that came up while reading this manual and to get clarity on your initial project focus.

§6 What a good week looks like

There is no single template for a productive week in a computational lab. Projects vary, timelines vary, and the nature of the work shifts between phases of exploration, analysis, and writing. Certain habits, though, characterize work that compounds well over time.

  • Eight hours of focused work per day. Computational science rewards sustained concentration. Fragmented time produces fragmented work.
  • Clear milestones and task completion. Know what you are trying to accomplish this week, and finish the things you start. Partial analyses are hard to hand off and easy to lose track of.
  • Daily commits and pushes. Code that is not on GitHub cannot be reviewed, helped with, or built on. See Document 03.
  • Well-documented code. Write it as if someone else will need to run it next week. They might. See Document 04.
  • Reading. Set aside time each week to read, across both computational methods and biology. The two must develop together. Methods without biology produce technically competent but scientifically hollow analysis.
  • Communication. Share what you are working on. Keep the PI and your colleagues updated. Ask questions when you are stuck.
  • Availability. Be reachable on Slack during working hours and responsive to questions from colleagues who may need your help or input.
On knowing what your code is doing

Reliability in computational science means code that produces exactly what you say it produces, and where you have verified that this is the case. No one will check your work automatically. The responsibility of knowing precisely what your analysis is doing (why each step is there, whether the output is what you expect, and whether that expectation is the right choice) rests entirely with you.

This judgment develops over time. Early on, it means slowing down to check intermediate outputs, reading documentation carefully, and asking whether the numbers you are seeing make biological sense. Over time it becomes intuition, and developing it is one of the most important parts of becoming a skilled computational scientist.

§7 Being a good colleague

Communicate your work

The PI cannot help with what they cannot see. Communicating regularly about what you are working on, in your weekly 1:1 and at lab meetings, is how scientific problems get noticed before they become costly, and how the team learns from your work. Regular communication is how a computational team functions.

Help others troubleshoot

One of the most valuable things you can do for a colleague is point them to work that already exists on GitHub, to a vignette that addresses their question, or to a helper function that solves their problem. The lab's collective infrastructure is only useful if people know it exists and can access it. Be generous with what you know.

Ask for help early

Do not spend more time than necessary stuck on a problem alone. Use Claude, documentation, and search first. If that does not resolve it, ask on Slack or in your 1:1. Asking for help early is how science moves faster. The cost of staying stuck silently is always higher than the cost of asking.

Finish what you start

Take ownership of your projects. That means completing analyses you have begun, not starting things you cannot finish, and not taking over someone else's work without explicit agreement. It also means being generous: share your methods, your functions, and your findings openly within the lab and, when the time is right, with the wider scientific community.

§8 The four steps of data science

A useful frame for thinking about computational work is the following progression. Every analysis, regardless of scale or complexity, passes through these four stages.

Step 1
Acquisition
Measurements. What data do we have, how was it collected, and what are its properties?
→
Step 2
Exploration
Observations. What patterns, structures, and anomalies does the data contain?
→
Step 3
Analysis
Statistics. What can be formally measured, tested, and concluded from these patterns?
→
Step 4
Communication
Story. How do we explain what was found to others, clearly and honestly?

Iteration between steps is normal, and these stages are not always strictly sequential in practice. Losing track of which step you are in, and jumping to conclusions before adequately exploring the data, is one of the most common sources of error in computational biology. Return to this frame whenever a project feels muddled.

§9 Things worth remembering

These are observations that tend to change how people approach the work. They are worth reading again at different stages of a project.

"Things take longer than you expect."
Every analysis, every tool, every paper. Build this into how you plan. A project that would take two weeks in an ideal world tends to take four in practice. Knowing this going in prevents unnecessary stress and missed deadlines.
"Organization is the difference between completion and failure."
The file naming conventions, the Git workflow, the header blocks in scripts: these are what make it possible to find things, hand things off, and finish them. A disorganized project rarely gets completed. An organized one almost always does.
"There is no substitute for knowing your data."
Understanding the structure of your data — its dimensions, its distributions, its quirks and failure modes — is the foundation of everything. No statistical method compensates for analysis run on data that has not been adequately explored. Step 2 (Exploration) is never skippable.
"Use GitHub."
Code that is not committed and pushed does not exist for anyone but you. Pushing daily is the minimum viable practice for working in a team.
"If you can visualize it, you can measure it."
Visualization is a step in analysis. When you can represent a pattern visually, you have understood it well enough to formalize it. If you cannot visualize what you are trying to measure, the conceptual work is not yet complete.
